Finding a **cheap electric car** is no longer a headache in 2026: the French market now lines up around ten models under 25,000 euros, several of them under the 20,000 euro mark. This comparison covers the best new and used options, without sacrificing range or reliability along the way.

**Cheap electric car: the essentials**

- The Dacia Spring remains the cheapest on the market, from 16,900 euros new.
- Around ten new models sit under 25,000 euros in 2026.
- Used, expect to pay from 6,000 to 10,000 euros for a reliable electric city car.
- The ecological bonus can cut several thousand euros off the price.
- A real-world range of 200 to 300 km covers the daily needs of most drivers.

## Cheap electric car: what changed in 2026

Just a few years ago, going electric was reserved for comfortable budgets. That is no longer true: cheaper-to-produce **LFP** batteries, competition from Chinese manufacturers and the expanding Dacia lineup have pushed entry prices down sharply. In 2026, the **Dacia Spring** remains the undisputed reference of the budget segment, starting at **16,900 euros** in the Essential 70 hp trim, with a 24.3 kWh battery delivering 225 km WLTP range in mixed cycle, and up to 315 km in strictly urban use.

Right behind it, the **Leapmotor T03** (around 17,900 euros) and the **BYD Dolphin Surf** (around 19,990 euros) confirm that the 20,000 euro mark is no longer a ceiling but an entry point for several credible models. Beyond this first tier, the choice widens considerably with more versatile cars, capable of leaving the city without struggling.

The **Renault 5 E-Tech** illustrates the segment's upmarket shift well: under 25,000 euros, it offers a 40 kWh battery for around 312 km of range, in a body style that won over the 2025 Car of the Year jury. The **Hyundai Inster**, at a comparable price, pushes range up to 370 km WLTP, one of the best figures in the whole cheap electric car category. The **Citroën ë-C3** focuses instead on family-friendly space with its 44 kWh LFP battery, while the **MG4** rounds things off with up to 450 km WLTP for a price that stays under 30,000 euros, a range-to-price ratio that is hard to match.

## Running costs: the other half of the price equation

The purchase price only tells half the story. A **cheap electric car** also costs less to run than an equivalent petrol city car: home charging averages 2 to 3 euros per 100 km, against 8 to 10 euros of fuel for a petrol city car. Over 12,000 km a year, the annual gap often exceeds 700 to 800 euros, an amount that quickly offsets part of the extra upfront cost compared with a comparable petrol model.

**Maintenance** follows the same downward logic: no oil change, no clutch, brake pads that wear more slowly thanks to regenerative braking. Manufacturer services generally stay cheaper than on a petrol car, even though specialised battery labour can occasionally weigh on the bill in case of a fault outside warranty. This usage gap explains why the **total cost of ownership** calculation often favours electric, even on an entry-level model, as long as daily trips stay within the real-world range.

## The used market: the best cheap electric car deals

To bring the budget down further, buying used remains the most effective route. Early-generation **Dacia Spring** and **Renault Zoe** models now change hands for between **6,000 and 6,500 euros**, a price level that brings electric close to a classic used petrol city car. The **Peugeot e-208**, available from around 10,000 euros, and the **Nissan Leaf II**, from 8,000 euros, offer between 270 and 395 km of WLTP range depending on version and year, more than enough to cover daily urban and suburban use.

On this market, the real focus should be battery health rather than mileage alone, a habit detailed in the [electric car range guide](/en/blog/electric-car-range/): asking for the state-of-health certificate (SoH) reveals the remaining capacity before signing. The usual used-car precautions still apply too: service history, number of previous owners and an up-to-date roadworthiness test make all the difference, as covered in the [complete guide to buying a used car](/en/blog/buying-used-car-guide/).

## Range, charging and reliability: what not to sacrifice

Choosing a **cheap electric car** should not come at the expense of real-world use. The average French driver covers less than 50 km a day, a need comfortably covered by the 200 to 320 km of real-world range offered by the cheapest city cars on the market. The calculation changes for regular motorway trips, where speed and cold weather noticeably cut into the advertised range: it is then worth looking at more range-focused models, like the ones compared in the [BYD Seal versus Tesla Model 3](/en/blog/byd-seal-vs-tesla-model-3/) match-up, built for longer distances.

**Charging speed** also deserves attention, often overlooked next to the range figure alone. Entry-level models generally settle for a more modest fast-charge rate, around 30 to 40 minutes to go from 20 to 80%, against 15 to 20 minutes on more premium models like those compared between [Tesla Model 3 and Model Y](/en/blog/tesla-model-3-vs-model-y/). For mostly urban use with charging at home or at work, this detail matters little; it becomes decisive for anyone who regularly drives long distances.

## Ecological bonus and purchase incentives: how much they change the price

The sticker price is not always the price paid. The **ecological bonus**, reserved for new vehicles under a price cap and subject to income conditions, can cut several thousand euros off the bill for an eligible model. The **conversion premium**, which can sometimes be combined with the bonus, targets lower-income households scrapping an older polluting vehicle. Several **local authorities** (regions, metropolitan areas) sometimes add a complementary incentive, subject to residency conditions: a quick check on the French ecological transition agency's website confirms eligibility before signing an order form. On a Dacia Spring or a Leapmotor T03 already at the bottom of the price range, stacking these incentives can bring the real cost well below the catalogue price.
